Westinghouse’s Failures for Workers and the Community

November 6, 2026

Westinghouse Electric Company has announced a roughly $1 billion expansion of its nuclear fuel manufacturing facility in Hopkins, South Carolina. The deal was approved by Governor McMaster on October 21, 2026. WCBD News reported that “[t]he expansion at Westinghouse’s Bluff Road campus in Hopkins will significantly increase production of low-enriched uranium plus, or LEU+, fuel” (Renaud, 2026). The company claims the project will add jobs, though it has not specified how many positions will be created by the projected 2030 completion date.

There is another, more important part of the announcement: public support for private investment. Richland County Council approved a 40-year tax incentive agreement for the project. The agreement reduces the facility’s taxable assessment ratio from 10.5% to 4%, freezes its property tax rate, and provides an additional tax credit during the first four years. Westinghouse must invest at least $400 million in qualifying property and equipment to receive the incentive (WIS, 2026). The public is being asked to supply tax concessions, infrastructure, and workers while Westinghouse supplies an investment on which it expects a profitable return. If the expansion fails to deliver the promised economic benefits, the public may be left with lost tax revenue and other costs without receiving the benefits it was promised.

Westinghouse plans to build the new factory next to its existing Hopkins facility. That facility was built in 1968 and produces around 10% of the fuel for the U.S. electric grid. The new facility will eventually make LEU+ fuel, which increases core energy density and allows power plants to extend intervals between refueling cycles, lowering long-term operational costs. The existing facility employs over 1,200 people in Lower Richland. According to a study by the University of South Carolina’s Darla Moore School of Business in collaboration with Westinghouse, construction and day-to-day operation will “have a cumulative economic impact of approximately $3.8 billion in Richland County by the year 2035,” with a projected $4.9 billion impact across South Carolina over the next decade. The study also states that the average salary at the factory is 64% higher than the South Carolina average of $59,000. But Westinghouse has not committed to hiring a specific number of new workers, nor has it disclosed the median salary at the factory, a better indicator of what an average worker will actually earn.

State and local governments have announced the deal with fanfare, framing it as a huge win for Richland County. Several politicians on both sides of the aisle have praised it. Governor McMaster, Congressman Clyburn, and Richland County Council Chairwoman Mackey have released statements ranging from groveling deference to euphoric proclamations of state pride. Chairwoman Mackey’s statement is especially revealing: “Richland County has spent years laying the groundwork for investments like this one, and we’re grateful to Westinghouse for this historic expansion … Our workforce has supported this facility for generations, and this expansion ensures that we’ll continue to see high-paying jobs and economic stability for years to come.” The rhetoric presents the corporation as the benefactor and the public as the beneficiary of its generosity. In reality, the public is underwriting a private project.

The V.C. Summer precedent matters because this is not a hypothetical risk. In 2008, Westinghouse partnered with SCE&G to build two nuclear reactors at the V.C. Summer plant in Jenkinsville, South Carolina. “The trio of companies estimated that the project would cost $9.8 billion and that both units would be operational by no later than 2018” (Neeley & Hyden, 2019). Instead, construction faced delays and cost overruns. In 2017, Westinghouse filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy—a year before the reactors were to be finished. The project was abandoned, ultimately costing $25 billion.

How did Westinghouse pay for this failure? Through the Base Load Review Act, which allowed utility companies to charge customers for the costs of new expansions before they were even built. The legislation forced ratepayers to cover not only the original estimate but all cost overruns. As Neeley and Hyden explain, “[t]he rates that a utility is allowed to charge are a function of its costs—the more money a utility spends, the more it is permitted to recoup from ratepayers.” This creates a perverse incentive structure: energy companies are rewarded for starting large, costly projects because they are guaranteed profit regardless of outcome. State regulators approve rates guaranteeing seven to nine percent profit on such endeavors; in South Carolina, SCE&G’s approved rate of return on equity was 10.25 percent. For the V.C. Summer project, customers were charged over an 18% increase in their electric bills, totaling $1.4 billion in recoup costs, approximately $1,500 per household for a plant that was never finished.

Meanwhile, Westinghouse’s Hopkins facility generates at least $711 million in profits annually. Yet consumers are forced to pay higher rates to help a profitable company “recoup costs” for failed ventures, unable to find recourse or compensation. Even more damning: the partially completed reactors sit empty despite their viability. “In South Carolina, members of the Governor’s Nuclear Advisory Council visited Virgil C. Summer Nuclear Power Station in September 2024 to explore whether its two cancelled Westinghouse AP1000 units could be redeveloped; they concluded that ‘no obvious conditions preclude undertaking completion’” (Hughes, 2026). Instead of finishing what was started, Westinghouse has shifted focus to expanding its Hopkins facility.

This is not only a local story about one company. It is part of a broader pattern in which local governments compete to attract mobile capital through development projects, incentives, and public-private partnerships. In his essay “From Managerialism to Entrepreneurialism,” David Harvey describes how local governments have increasingly shifted toward entrepreneurial strategies to attract investment. The amount of private investment in a city becomes a measurement of governmental success, even if the terms of that investment leave the public with substantial costs and risks. Recent research supports this critique. In a 2025 study, Dr. Cailin Slattery found that “competition increases welfare (total economic surplus) by about 4% over a counter-factual subsidy ban, but this surplus is captured entirely by the firms” (Slattery, 2025). Most of the supposed economic benefits of deals like the Hopkins expansion are captured by the private firm, not by the community. This is how a city like Columbia can have a multibillion-dollar factory while still not having benches at many of its bus stops. Related research on subsidy competition also finds that the cost of municipal debt in the secondary market increases for winning counties compared to losing counties, and that counties with high debt capacity issue additional debt. Counties end up taking on more debt to finance what is ultimately a private venture for profit.

A key aspect of attracting private investment lies in the development of cultural and consumer centers, which can make a city more appealing to corporations. Convention centers, stadiums, and shopping malls are built not just for small businesses to use, but as a signpost that says: “we are a modern, exciting city! Please invest here!” How many of these places can we build before every city looks the same and the competitive advantage is gone? Harvey writes that the result is a “stimulating if often destructive maelstrom of urban-based cultural, political, production and consumption innovations,” marked by a “penchant for design of urban fragments rather than comprehensive urban planning,” for “ephemerality and eclecticism of fashion and style rather than the search for enduring values,” and finally “for medium over message and image over substance.” These deals not only take away public funds; they slowly suck out city culture and pride. The modern city has little meaning other than as a way to generate capital.

Westinghouse also has a long history of suppressing worker organizing. In 2024, 600 of 900 employees petitioned to join the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers; two-thirds of the workforce signed authorization cards, far exceeding the 30% threshold required by federal law. South Carolina’s right-to-work status allowed Westinghouse to outright refuse recognition without a vote. The company held mandatory anti-union meetings, distributed fliers with draft emails for employees to withdraw their authorization cards, and brought in union-avoidance consultants paid $475 per hour plus travel expenses. In January 2024, the company continued to hold anti-union meetings and handed out an anti-union flier that included a draft email for employees to withdraw any union authorization card they may have signed. The plant manager explicitly urged workers to “vote NO!” on company time.

Westinghouse also failed to file required LM-20 forms disclosing its persuader activities. After months of anti-union efforts, with at least a dozen allegations that the company violated federal law, Westinghouse defeated employees’ attempts to unionize in a March 2024 election. Those Unfair Labor Practice charges continued to work their way through the National Labor Relations Board more than a year after the election. The IBEW has charged Westinghouse with violating the National Labor Relations Act through soliciting grievances and making promises to fix them, granting benefits, banning union literature, threatening employees, and terminating an employee for union activity. Westinghouse spent roughly $1 million on union-busting—including at least $800,000 documented by the Private Equity Stakeholder Project—yet cannot compensate customers for subsidies extracted from their electric bills for failed reactors. The message is clear: workers’ collective power is the threat, and the company will spend millions to crush it.

Because the Hopkins facility cannot be moved easily, it is also a strategic site for union organizing. But Westinghouse’s treatment of workers goes beyond union avoidance. Its disregard for worker safety is documented. In Skinner v. Westinghouse Electric Company (2011), an employee who had worked at the plant since 1968 developed chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asbestosis, occupational bronchitis, severe sleep apnea, and sinusitis from regular exposure to asbestos dust and toxic chemicals. Skinner was awarded no compensation because Westinghouse argued he had not lost wages, even though his pulmonary disease appeared after leaving the company and was directly caused by unsafe working conditions.

More recently, on April 1, 2025, a Columbia Fuel Fabrication Facility employee was sprayed with ammoniated water from a leaking chemical hose, according to Westinghouse’s own report to regulators. In 2021, the EPA expressed concern about “incidents and violations” during the past 15 years at Westinghouse, including an accident in August in which a plant worker suffered chemical burns and was contaminated with radioactive material (Fretwell, 2021). Fretwell also noted groundwater pollution that could spread to Congaree National Park, caused by Westinghouse. These are not isolated accidents; they are the predictable result of a company that treats workers as disposable inputs.

Westinghouse exemplifies what Marxist political economy identifies as the fundamental contradiction of capitalism: the private ownership of socially necessary infrastructure for the purpose of profit maximization rather than human need. Under capitalism, when enterprises fail, losses are socialized, borne by workers and communities, while profits remain privatized. The Base Load Review Act institutionalized this dynamic: ratepayers fund corporate ventures with guaranteed returns, absorbing all risk while Westinghouse collects profit regardless of outcome. The $1.4 billion extracted from South Carolina households for unbuilt reactors represents a direct transfer of wealth from the working class to a private equity-owned corporation (Brookfield Asset Management).

David Harvey (1989) notes that under capitalism, “it is the broad range of class practices connected to the circulation of capital, the reproduction of labour power and class relations, and the need to control labour power, that remains hegemonic.” Westinghouse’s aggressive union-busting- spending roughly $1 million to defeat a union with overwhelming worker support—demonstrates this imperative. Workers are not partners but costs to be minimized; their collective power must be crushed to maintain managerial control and maximize extraction of surplus value. The Skinner case and the 2025 chemical leak show how worker safety is subordinated to production. When workers cannot organize, they cannot demand safer conditions. Westinghouse’s refusal to recognize the IBEW is not merely an anti-union tactic; it is a mechanism for maintaining the power to expose workers to asbestos, toxic chemicals, and radioactive contamination without effective challenge. The state’s right-to-work laws and Governor McMaster’s hostility to unions—“We will fight all the way to the gates of hell” (Holdman, 2024)—demonstrate how legal and political institutions protect capital’s control over labor.

South Carolina’s right-to-work laws, the Base Load Review Act, and the state’s response to the V.C. Summer collapse show the state’s role in protecting corporate power against workers and communities. The legal and regulatory apparatus functions not to protect the public but to ensure capital’s continued accumulation. Workers who petition for a union are met with mandatory anti-union meetings; ratepayers who fund failed projects have no avenue to refuse.

Electricity is not a luxury but a necessity for modern life. Westinghouse and similar utilities operate as private monopolies: consumers cannot choose alternative providers, cannot boycott effectively, and cannot opt out. You cannot ask the average South Carolinian whether they would be willing to go without electricity to boycott Westinghouse’s practices. This captive market eliminates the consumer “choice” that capitalism claims justifies private enterprise. The EPA’s concerns about radioactive contamination, groundwater pollution threatening Congaree National Park, and worker exposure to toxic chemicals represent what ecological socialists call “externalities”, costs dumped onto workers, communities, and ecosystems rather than internalized by the corporation.

The privatization of electricity does not benefit the people. Westinghouse’s expansion will increase production without guaranteeing good jobs, worker safety, or community benefit. The costs will again be offloaded onto consumers and workers.

Immediate actions:

  • Vote in midterms and primaries for candidates committed to utility regulation and worker rights; early voting is already underway.
  • Educate yourself and others about how electric bills function, how “rate of return” policies transfer wealth from consumers to corporations, and how right-to-work laws weaken worker power.
  • Organize locally around the Hopkins expansion and any new industrial projects; demand transparency about jobs, safety records, and who pays.
  • Attend local council meetings, support workers’ right to organize, and refuse to allow projects that harm workers and communities. Disrupt the disrupters.

Electricity should not be controlled by private companies seeking profit. Communities and local governments should regulate and build infrastructure to meet human needs, not corporate demands. Public ownership, whether municipal utilities, cooperatives, or state enterprises, would eliminate the profit motive that drives cost overruns, union-busting, and unsafe working conditions.

Workers at Westinghouse must be allowed to unionize so they can advocate for safer conditions and have a democratic voice in decisions affecting their communities. Ideally, workers would share in the profits they create and have decision-making power over production. This behemoth that harms us and continues the cycle of poverty must be leashed. But more than reform, we need transformation: a society where electricity, water, and the environment are treated as commons to be protected, not commodities to be exploited, and where workers, not private equity firms. hold power over the work they do. Let’s band together, not just to keep this dog in check, but to build a world where such dogs no longer roam.
